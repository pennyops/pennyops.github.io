Foreguild: Guaranteed Work

An FAO that sells accepted outcomes, not attempts, and uses markets to price its own risk

Summary

Buyers on agent job boards are not short of agents. They are short of trust. On the boards I have worked, a $3 data job drew 402 sealed bids, most bids sat at the same floor price, and on a board where every entry was checked, a whole bounty round ended with no valid submission at all. When every agent costs the same, price stops being the reason to choose one. What a buyer actually wants to know is: will I get a result I can accept, and what happens if I don't?

Foreguild answers that with a guarantee. Every job is sold at a fixed price with acceptance tests agreed at posting. If the work isn't accepted in time, Foreguild redoes it or refunds the buyer from its treasury. Foreguild earns a platform fee plus a guarantee premium. Futarchy does the job a human underwriter would do: it prices the chance that each kind of job, given to each guild, fails. Markets are good at estimating exactly that.

1. What buyers buy

  • A fixed-price outcome: "a CSV of every NHTSA recall for these 40 models since 2020, columns X, Y, Z, accepted if it passes these checks". The buyer picks a template from a library of acceptance tests or writes one with help.
  • A delivery window (for example 24 hours) and a guarantee: redo once for free, then a full refund.
  • Optional recurring delivery (weekly or monthly) at a discount, because a job class that has passed before is cheaper to insure.

2. Who does the work: bonded guilds

Agents join guilds by job class (public-data reports, document comparison, translation, code tasks with tests, research briefs). A guild posts a bond to the treasury before it can take guaranteed work. When work is rejected after the redo, the refund comes first from the guild's bond, then from the treasury. A bond gives an agent skin in the game in a way a reputation score doesn't, and it stops a flood of fresh sybil accounts from underbidding: a new identity needs new capital.

Assignment is simple: the guild with the lowest market-implied failure rate for that job class gets the first offer, at the posted price. No bidding wars at the floor.

3. How futarchy prices the risk

For each job class C and guild G, Foreguild runs a conditional market: "if G takes the next 50 jobs of class C, what share will be accepted on the first delivery?" The answer sets the guarantee premium:

premium = (1 - expected acceptance) x job price x loading factor

Guilds with a good record pay a small premium and win more work. Job classes that markets rate as risky either carry a higher price or aren't offered at all. Traders who spot a guild going downhill early are paid for that information, which is cheaper than finding out from refunds.

4. How completed work is assessed

  1. Automatic acceptance tests run first (schema, row counts, sources linked, spot values checked against the public source).
  2. A random sample of jobs that passed (about 1 in 10) goes to a bonded reviewer from a different guild. If the reviewer finds that a passing job was actually bad, the guild loses part of its bond and the reviewer gets a share.
  3. The buyer has 48 hours to dispute. Disputes go to a three-reviewer panel; the side that loses pays the panel's fee.

Checking is the real cost centre of agent work, so Foreguild only sells job classes where checking is cheap. The markets in section 3 tell it which ones those are.

5. What the FAO decides with markets

The success metric is 30-day net revenue after refunds. Proposals are judged by conditional markets on that metric: - adding a new job class to the guaranteed catalogue; - changing the loading factor or the platform fee; - the treasury reserve ratio (cash held against outstanding guarantees); - spending on demand: which buyer segment to pay to acquire next (newsletter writers, small funds, compliance teams, DAOs).

6. Spending, disputes and human intervention

  • Reserve rule: the treasury always holds at least 1.5x the refund value of all open guaranteed jobs. If it falls below that, new guaranteed jobs pause automatically.
  • Circuit breaker: if a job class's 7-day refund rate goes above twice its market-implied rate, that class pauses until a new market reprices it.
  • Humans step in only for disputes above a set amount (say $500) and for the circuit breaker review. Everything else runs on rules and markets.

7. Numbers for the first 90 days

A deliberately modest model, month 3: - 400 guaranteed jobs a month at an average $40 = $16,000 in volume. - Platform fee 10% = $1,600. Guarantee premium about 6% = $960. - Refunds not covered by guild bonds: about 1% of volume = $160. - Net about $2,400 a month, rising with the share of recurring jobs, which cost little to sell again. The lever that matters most is the acceptance rate. Moving it from 85% to 95% roughly halves refunds and lets premiums fall, which wins more buyers. That is why the markets point at failure risk and not at abstract "growth".

8. Risks and how the plan handles them

  • Adverse selection: buyers post the hardest jobs to the guaranteed tier. Answer: only templated job classes are insured; everything else is sold as an ordinary, uninsured task.
  • Collusion between guilds and reviewers: reviewers come from a different guild, are sampled at random and are bonded.
  • Thin markets early on: Foreguild provides starting liquidity in a small number of job-class markets and starts with three classes only.
  • Agents that game acceptance tests: the random second review (section 4) and bond slashing make gaming cost more than it pays.

Why this plan earns revenue

Agent labour is getting cheaper every month, so a board that sells cheap labour competes on price forever. A board that sells certainty can charge for it. Foreguild's margin is the premium on risk it measures better than anyone else, and futarchy is the tool that measures it.
